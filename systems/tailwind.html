System 16 · Evidence base frozen since 2020

Tailwind

Active travel evidence, brought up to date

England decides where to invest in walking and cycling using commuting patterns recorded in 2011. Scotland built a maintained replacement. England did not.

TransportEquityCentral government
The problem

The standard evidence tool for active travel business cases in England is built on 2011 Census commuting data. Its data repositories were last updated in May 2020, and its geography codes are still the 2011 set. It has never been rebuilt on the 2021 Census.

So a 2026 English cycling scheme is justified using how people travelled to work fifteen years ago — before the pandemic reshaped commuting entirely, and before a decade of housing development changed where people live.

Scotland faced the same problem and solved it, building a maintained, open-source successor on current mapping data, updated in 2025 and actively developed since. There is no English equivalent. Active Travel England publishes design and scheme review tools, which are different instruments entirely.

The supporting data is in similar condition. There is no national dataset for footway condition — road condition statistics cover carriageways only, and effectively one council publishes footway surveys openly. There is no national crossing dataset and no national register of school streets.

Meanwhile the cycle network data itself is genuinely excellent: an open licence, weekly updates, 37,208 segments with surface, lighting and traffic-free attributes. The network is well mapped. The demand model is fifteen years old.

The system

Tailwind rebuilds active travel demand evidence on current data, at the geography where schemes are actually designed.

It starts by replacing the 2011 commuting base with 2021 Census travel patterns, current population, and the development that has happened since — which is where the platform’s completion signal earns its place.

It joins that to the network data that already exists and is well maintained, so route quality, surface, lighting and traffic separation inform demand rather than sitting in a separate map.

And it fills the pedestrian gap as far as open data allows, being explicit that footway condition is not nationally collected. Where the only national source is volunteer-maintained mapping, that is stated rather than obscured.

Benefits

For government

  • Replaces a fifteen-year-old evidence base underpinning current investment decisions.
  • Gives Active Travel England the demand analysis its design and review tools assume but do not provide.
  • Brings England level with Scotland, which already has a maintained open successor.
  • Makes development-aware active travel planning possible, which the frozen tool cannot support.

For the public

  • Investment targeted at where people would walk and cycle now, not where they commuted in 2011.
  • New developments assessed for active travel access as they are built.
  • Communities can see the evidence behind schemes proposed in their area.

Risks & mitigations
Pandemic-affected inputs

Census 2021 travel-to-work data was collected under pandemic conditions and is flagged by the statistics authority. Using it as a normal-times baseline would repeat the existing tool’s error in a new direction. Treatment must be explicit.

Share-alike licensing

The only national footway and crossing data carries share-alike obligations that would attach to a derived database. Used for analysis and gap-finding, never shipped in the product.

Being overtaken

Government could fund an English successor directly. That would be the right outcome nationally, so the work is positioned to inform it rather than depend on its absence.
